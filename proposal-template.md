# Proposal Template

This template can be used for either a thesis proposal or a project proposal. The same overall structure works for both, but the emphasis may differ. A project proposal often focuses more on feasibility, implementation, and evaluation, while a thesis proposal usually gives more attention to literature positioning, research questions, and contribution.

## 1. Project or thesis title
- Write a concise and informative title.
- The title should communicate the core problem, method, or domain.
- Example: "Human Activity Recognition for Rehabilitation Monitoring Using Skeleton-Based Deep Learning"

## 2. Introduction

### Problem statement and motivation
- Explain the problem in language a reader can understand.
- Describe why the issue matters to the field, to practice, or to a real-world application.
- Briefly explain why the problem is worth studying now.
- Focus on the gap, opportunity, or need rather than only on the technical method.

### Background and literature review
- Summarize the relevant literature and prior work.
- Identify what is already known and what remains unresolved.
- Explain how the proposed work connects to the broader area.
- Cite the key papers or methods that define the area and highlight the gap.
- State clearly what literature suggests and what is still missing.

### Research gap and proposed direction
- Based on the literature, describe the specific gap your work will address.
- Explain why this project is a meaningful next step.
- Briefly mention the overall approach you intend to take.

## 3. Research questions or objectives
- State the main question or questions to be addressed.
- Include 2-4 clear objectives if appropriate.
- Keep the questions realistic, specific, and testable.

## 4. Proposed approach and methods
- Describe the data, methods, models, algorithms, or frameworks to be used.
- Explain why this approach is suitable for the problem.
- Include the expected workflow from data preparation through analysis.
- Be concrete about assumptions and the logic behind the chosen design.

## 5. Data, resources, and feasibility
- Describe the dataset, software, sensors, equipment, or other resources required.
- Explain whether the data are already available, need to be collected, or will be obtained from a partner or public source.
- Be honest about time, access, compute, and supervision needs.

## 6. Evaluation plan
- Explain how success will be measured.
- Include evaluation metrics where relevant.
- If appropriate, describe baseline methods or comparison criteria.
- Define what counts as a successful outcome before finalizing the method description.

## 7. Expected contribution
- State what the project is expected to contribute to the field or to practice.
- Explain how the work is useful or improved compared with prior work.
- Avoid overstating claims; focus on a realistic contribution.

## 8. Risks, limitations, and contingency plans
- Identify possible issues such as limited data, difficult datasets, or methodological problems.
- Explain how the project would adapt if the planned approach does not work as expected.
- This section shows planning and maturity.

## 9. Timeline and milestones
- Break the work into phases.
- Include major tasks and expected timing.
- Example milestones: literature review, data preparation, baseline implementation, model development, evaluation, and writing.

## 10. Ethics, privacy, and responsible use
- If human data are involved, mention consent, privacy, confidentiality, and compliance requirements.
- If working with health or behavioural data, explain relevant safeguards.

## 11. References
- Include the main sources that informed the proposal.
- Use a consistent citation style and prioritize the most relevant sources.

## Short guidance

- Start with a clear problem, not only a method.
- Ground the proposal in the literature and explain the gap.
- Keep the scope realistic.
- Show both the research question and the evaluation plan.
- Write for a reader who is interested but may not know every technical detail.
